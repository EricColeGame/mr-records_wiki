Landing your music in front of the right ears is the hardest part of being an independent artist, and that is exactly the problem this MR Records demo guide is built to solve. A polished demo package will not guarantee a deal, but a careless one guarantees silence — and silence is what most unsigned artists get. Whether you are sending your first three-song EP or your tenth finished single, this MR Records demo guide covers what to prepare, where to send it, and how to follow up without burning a bridge.

## What A&R Teams Actually Listen For in the First 30 Seconds

Most decision-makers do not listen to your entire demo on the first pass. They hear the opening verse, maybe the chorus, and then they decide whether the rest deserves their time. That single reality shapes every recommendation in this MR Records demo guide.

| What gets weighed | Why it matters | What you control |
|---|---|---|
| Songwriting | Melody, hook, and lyric clarity carry a demo | Structure, hook placement, rewriting |
| Vocal identity | Distinct voices are easier to remember and pitch | Performance, tuning, arrangement |
| Production quality | Rough is fine; muddy is not | Mix balance, loudness, noise floor |
| Fit with the roster | A great song that does not fit still gets passed | Research the catalog first |
| Professionalism | File names and contact details signal intent | Naming conventions, clean metadata |

Spend time studying the label's existing catalog before you send anything. A catalog tells you which sounds the team already invests in, which collaborators they work with, and where your material might fit. Public databases such as the [Discogs label directory](https://www.discogs.com/) make that research quick, and they are free to use.

The practical takeaway is simple: lead with your strongest song. If your best track sits third on the list, move it to the front. Nobody awards points for track order loyalty, and a strong opener buys you the next thirty seconds.

## Building a Demo Package That Survives the First Cut

A demo package is not a press kit, an EPK, or a full brand launch. It is a small, clean bundle that lets someone hear your music in under two minutes and contact you in under ten seconds. The next stage of this MR Records demo guide focuses on the package itself, because packaging is the part artists most often rush.

| Step | What to do | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Choose two or three songs | Pick your strongest, most finished tracks | Respects the listener's time |
| 2. Export clean files | Provide lossless WAV plus a streaming-friendly MP3 | Avoids conversion problems |
| 3. Name files clearly | ArtistName_TrackTitle_Mix.mp3 | Signals professionalism |
| 4. Write a one-page bio | Who you are, where you are from, what you have released | Gives context without a novel |
| 5. Add working links | Streaming profile, video, socials | Lets them verify you instantly |
| 6. Include contact info | Email and phone in the message body | Prevents a dead end |

Keep the bio to a single page and use bullet points for achievements rather than long paragraphs. Community reports from unsigned artists consistently suggest that short, focused packages get opened far more often than sprawling ones.

A solid asset list looks like this:

- Two or three finished songs, mixed and mastered where possible
- One high-resolution press photo with the photographer credited
- A short bio in plain text, not a locked PDF
- Streaming links tested on a phone before you send them
- A lyric sheet if your writing is your selling point

If you are unsure whether a mix is ready, get one honest opinion from someone who is not a close friend. Encouragement feels good; accurate feedback gets you signed.

## Choosing the Right Way to Send Your Demo

There is no single universal submission door. Labels change their intake policies, close portals, and reopen them without much notice, so treat every channel as something to verify rather than assume. This part of the MR Records demo guide is about matching the channel to your situation.

| Channel | Best for | What to expect | Tip |
|---|---|---|---|
| Official submission form or portal | Artists following the stated process | Slowest, most structured | Follow instructions exactly |
| Direct email to an A&R or info address | Artists with a referral or genuine connection | Low response rate | One email, one link, no oversized attachments |
| Showcases and industry nights | Performers who want a live impression | Immediate feedback and real conversations | Bring a QR code to your music |
| Playlist and DSP pitching tools | Building independent momentum | Not a label submission | Use it to create proof of traction |
| Referrals from managers or producers | Artists with industry contacts | Highest hit rate | Ask for a warm intro, not a favor |

Before you send anything, confirm the current process through the label's official channels. Anything advertising a paid shortcut straight to an A&R desk deserves heavy skepticism, and paying for access is rarely how real deals start.

Do not blast five different channels at once either. Pick the one that fits your situation, send it well, and keep a simple spreadsheet of what you sent and when.

## Five Demo Mistakes That Quietly Kill Your Chances

Most of the failures covered in this MR Records demo guide come down to a handful of habits rather than a lack of talent. These are the ones that show up again and again.

| Mistake | Why it hurts | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Sending ten unfinished tracks | Signals you cannot self-edit | Send your best two or three |
| Generic mass email | Reads as spam and gets deleted | Personalize the first line |
| Huge attachments | Can bounce or get filtered | Share links instead |
| No contact details | Ends the conversation early | Put email and phone in the message body |
| Following up daily | Turns interest into irritation | Space out your follow-ups |

Two more worth naming: do not lead with a cover song unless the arrangement is genuinely yours, and do not apologize for your demo before anyone has heard it. Confidence is part of the package, and hedging in your first sentence sets the wrong tone for everything that follows.

## Follow-Up Etiquette After You Hit Send

The follow-up is where artists either look organized or look desperate, and as this MR Records demo guide keeps emphasizing, tone matters as much as timing. A reasonable rule of thumb — not a label policy — is to wait roughly two weeks, then send one short, polite check-in.

| Time since sending | Action | Tone |
|---|---|---|
| Day 0 | Send the demo with links and contact info | Direct and brief |
| Week 2 | One short check-in email | Friendly, no pressure |
| Week 6 | Share a genuine update such as a new release or growing numbers | Informative |
| Month 3 and beyond | Move on and keep releasing music | Professional |

If you receive a pass, thank them and ask one question: is there anything you would suggest I work on? Plenty of industry people answer that honestly, and the feedback is often worth more than the rejection stings.

Above all, keep releasing. A demo that gets ignored this year can open a door next year once your catalog, live show, or listener numbers have grown.

## FAQ

**How many songs should I send in an MR Records demo guide submission?**
Two or three finished tracks is the standard advice. Lead with the song you would play for someone if you only had one chance to make an impression.

**Does this MR Records demo guide apply to other labels?**
Yes. The core principles — short packages, clean files, verified submission channels, and respectful follow-up — transfer to almost any label, publisher, or sync agency you approach.

**Should I pay a service to submit my demo for me?**
Usually not. Paying for access to an A&R desk is a red flag, and most legitimate submission routes are either free or charge a small, publicly listed fee.

**What if I never hear back at all?**
Keep releasing music and building your own audience. Momentum you create independently is often what makes a label take a second look later.
