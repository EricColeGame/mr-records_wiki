## Grading and Condition: What the Labels Really Mean

Most independent shops grade used vinyl using the widely recognized Goldmine-style scale. It's worth knowing, because the difference between two grades can be a meaningful chunk of money. You can cross-reference pressing details, matrix numbers, and grading conventions on [Discogs, the standard database for record collectors](https://www.discogs.com), which is the reference most shops and buyers use when pricing used stock.

| Grade | Meaning | Typical Buying Advice |
|---|---|---|
| Mint (M) | Sealed or flawless, essentially unplayed | Rare for used stock; pay a premium only when it matters |
| Near Mint (NM) | Looks unplayed, may show one faint mark | The sweet spot for most collectors |
| Very Good Plus (VG+) | Minor surface marks, plays clean | Best value for everyday listening copies |
| Very Good (VG) | Visible wear, light surface noise | Fine for rare titles that never show up cleaner |
| Good (G) | Heavy wear, audible noise | Buy only as a placeholder |
| Poor (P) | Cracked, warped, or unplayable | Skip unless the cover alone is collectible |

Two rules keep grading honest. First, a grade describes the vinyl and the sleeve separately — a Near Mint record in a Very Good sleeve is common and should be priced accordingly. Second, always ask whether a used record has been play-graded or only visually graded. Visual grading is faster; play grading is more reliable, especially for anything you plan to spend real money on.

### What to Inspect in Sixty Seconds

- Hold the record at an angle under a light source.
- Look for deep scratches that catch a fingernail.
- Check for edge warping by sighting along the rim.
- Confirm the correct record is inside the correct sleeve.
- Read the label for pressing or reissue markings.

The bargain bin deserves special attention. It's where beginner collectors learn fast and cheap: a dollar or two per record means you can buy ten, play them, and discover what you actually like without much risk. The tradeoff is condition — expect noise, expect warps, and expect to pass on most of what you flip through.

Reissue racks serve a different purpose. Modern represses make classic albums available in sealed condition, which is ideal if you want a clean copy to play rather than an original pressing to preserve. Just don't assume a reissue is automatically inferior; mastering quality varies widely, and some reissues are widely regarded as excellent.

## Pricing, Trade-Ins, and Getting Fair Value

Used vinyl pricing is driven by three variables: condition, pressing, and demand. Understanding how those interact helps you decide when a price is fair and when to walk away.

| Budget Level | Realistic Haul | Strategy |
|---|---|---|
| $20 | Two to four used LPs | Bargain bins and VG/VG+ copies |
| $50 | Five to eight used LPs, or one new release | Mix one premium title with cheap discoveries |
| $100 | A focused mini-collection | Target one artist or genre and buy depth |
| Trade credit | Store credit instead of cash | Usually the better value if you'll shop again |

Trade-ins are where a relationship with the shop pays off. Most stores offer more in credit than in cash, because credit keeps the money circulating in the shop. Before you bring in a box, clean the records, match the correct sleeves, and separate anything that's genuinely damaged. A tidy, honest trade is remembered — and regulars often get first look at incoming stock as a result.

Two more pricing notes worth remembering. First, an original pressing isn't automatically worth more than a reissue; it depends on the specific release, the mastering, and collector demand. Second, "rare" gets thrown around loosely. If a title is available sealed and new at a reasonable price, it isn't rare — it's just old.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is Mr Records record shop good for beginners?**

Yes. A shop with organized sections and bargain bins is one of the cheapest ways to learn what you like. Start in the bargain bin, buy a handful of records, and play them at home before spending more on higher grades.

**What grade of vinyl should I buy for everyday listening?**

Very Good Plus is the practical sweet spot for most listeners. It usually plays cleanly, costs noticeably less than Near Mint, and is far easier to find for older titles that rarely appear in flawless condition.

**Should I buy original pressings or reissues?**

It depends on your goal. Reissues are typically sealed, clean, and easy to replace; originals can carry collector value and sometimes different mastering. If you want to listen, buy the best copy you can afford in the grade you trust.

**Can I trade records in at a shop like Mr Records record shop?**

Most independent shops accept trades, usually offering more in store credit than in cash. Clean your records, match the correct sleeves, and be upfront about condition — an accurate trade builds the kind of relationship that gets you first access to new arrivals.
